Chapter 1

Introduction & Brief History

A headphone is an electroacoustic transducer system worn on or in the ear that converts an electrical audio signal into sound pressure at the eardrum.

Unlike a loudspeaker, which excites a room, a headphone excites a tiny, highly individual acoustic space — the volume between the driver and your eardrum. Almost every design decision in this lesson flows from that single difference.

Milestones

The core lesson of history: headphone design has repeatedly been reshaped by use-case shifts (telephony → hi-fi → portable → mobile/wireless) more than by pure acoustics. A great headphone design always starts with "who is wearing this, where, and why."
Chapter 2

Sound Fundamentals & Psychoacoustics

2.1 Physical basics

  • Sound is a pressure wave. Frequency (Hz) maps to pitch, amplitude to loudness; human hearing spans roughly 20 Hz – 20 kHz (upper limit falls with age).
  • Sound Pressure Level (SPL) is measured in dB relative to 20 µPa. Every +10 dB is perceived as roughly "twice as loud"; +3 dB doubles acoustic power.
  • Wavelength matters: at 20 kHz, λ ≈ 17 mm — the same order as ear-canal and earcup dimensions. High-frequency response in headphones is dominated by resonances and standing waves in the ear coupling, and varies wildly between individuals and even between re-seats.

2.2 The outer ear is part of the speaker

In free air, the pinna, concha, and ear canal impose direction-dependent filtering — the Head-Related Transfer Function (HRTF). The ear canal itself is a ~25 mm closed pipe with a quarter-wave resonance near 2.5–3 kHz, giving humans peak sensitivity there.

A headphone bypasses part of this system: over-ears preserve most pinna interaction, while IEMs bypass the pinna entirely and must build the missing 3 kHz ear-gain into their tuning.

The single most important psychoacoustic concept in headphone design: the target response at the eardrum must recreate what a good sound source in a room would have produced after the body's own filtering.

2.3 Perceptual phenomena the designer must know

PhenomenonDesign consequence
Equal-loudness contours (ISO 226)Bass/treble need relatively more SPL at quiet volumes; explains "loudness" EQ and why quiet listening sounds thin.

3.2 Open-back vs. closed-back — the fundamental acoustic fork

Open-back. The rear of the driver radiates freely through a grille. The rear wave never reflects back through the diaphragm, so response is smoother and subjectively more "spacious." Cost: zero isolation, total leakage, and bass roll-off — the front volume can't hold static pressure (no pressure-chamber boost).
Closed-back. A sealed cup enables passive isolation and stronger low bass, but the rear wave reflects inside the cup, producing standing-wave colorations (typically 200 Hz–1 kHz) that must be tamed with damping materials and cup geometry.

Semi-open / vented: almost every real product sits on the continuum. Controlled leaks are tuning tools, not defects: a bass port relieves pressure and shapes sub-bass; a pad-to-head leak of a few mm² can cut 100 Hz output by several dB — the reason glasses wearers hear less bass on over-ears.

3.3 By use case (drives the whole spec)

  • Studio/reference: neutral target, durability, replaceable parts, low unit-to-unit variance, often high impedance.
  • Audiophile: resolution and timbre priorities; exotic drivers accepted; weight/price tolerance high.
  • Consumer wireless/ANC: DSP-defined sound, battery, mics, ANC depth, app ecosystem; acoustics designed with DSP correction assumed.
  • Stage/broadcast: isolation, fit security, cable robustness, sweat resistance.
  • Gaming/comms: mic quality, long-session comfort, low latency, positional cues.
  • Hearables/health: heart-rate sensors, hearing-assist DSP; regulatory overlap with medical devices.
Chapter 4

Transducer (Driver) Technologies

Each technology differs in how force is applied to the diaphragm — which dictates its distortion, bandwidth, and cost profile.

4.1 Dynamic (moving-coil) — ~95% of the market

Principle: a voice coil attached to the diaphragm apex sits in the annular gap of a permanent magnet; current produces Lorentz force, driving the diaphragm like a piston.

Strengths: cheap to make well; excellent bass (large excursion); high sensitivity — phone-friendly; robust; one driver covers 20 Hz–20 kHz.

Weaknesses: point-drive flexes the diaphragm at high frequencies (break-up modes → treble peaks); coil mass limits transients; motor nonlinearity creates bass harmonic distortion.

Design levers: diaphragm material/dome geometry, surround compliance (sets f₀ ≈ 60–120 Hz), rear-vent damping, magnet strength, CCAW coils.

4.2 Planar magnetic

A large, thin film carries a flat serpentine conductor across its whole surface; bar-magnet arrays apply force uniformly over the entire area — surface-drive rather than point-drive, so the diaphragm moves like an ideal piston.

  • Strengths: very low distortion (especially bass — no coil-in-gap nonlinearity); uniform transients; huge radiating area; flat resistive impedance.
  • Weaknesses: heavy magnet arrays (flagships historically 400–700 g); low sensitivity (needs a capable amp); costly tensioning/assembly; magnets partially obstruct the sound path.
  • Examples: Audeze LCD, HiFiMAN Susvara/Arya, Dan Clark Audio (AMTS resonator inserts), Meze Elite.

4.3 Electrostatic

An ultralight ~1–2 µm diaphragm charged to high DC bias (e.g., 580 V "Pro bias" for Stax) sits between two perforated stators carrying the amplified audio signal. Moving mass approaches that of the air itself.

  • Strengths: the lowest moving mass of any technology → extraordinary detail, vanishing mid/treble distortion.
  • Weaknesses: needs a dedicated high-voltage energizer; limited max SPL and bass slam; dust/humidity sensitivity; cost.
  • Examples: Stax SR-009S/X9000, Sennheiser HE-1, Audeze CRBN, Shure KSE1200 (electrostatic IEM).

4.4 Balanced armature (BA) — the IEM workhorse

A tiny reed balanced between magnets pivots with coil current; a drive rod couples it to a mini-diaphragm; sound exits through a spout. Hearing-aid heritage (Knowles, Sonion dominate supply). Tiny and efficient with superb mid/treble resolution — but narrow bandwidth per unit, so flagships stack multi-driver arrays with passive crossovers, tuned tube lengths, and color-coded acoustic dampers. Limited excursion means bass usually comes from vented BAs or a hybrid dynamic woofer.

4.5 Hybrids, tribrids & the rest

Hybrid/tribrid IEMs

Chapter 5

Acoustic Design of the Earcup System

The driver is perhaps a third of the acoustic story. The system — front volume, rear volume, vents, damping, pads, and the listener's anatomy — determines the final response.

5.1 The lumped-element mental model

At low frequencies (wavelength ≫ dimensions), the headphone behaves like an electrical circuit analog:

Compliances → capacitors
Front-volume air spring, rear-volume air spring, suspension, earpad foam.
Masses → inductors
Diaphragm + coil moving mass, air plugs in ports and vents.
Resistances → resistors
Damping meshes/foams, leaks, port losses.

Designers build SPICE-like network models (or use COMSOL, AKABAK, SonicSurf) before cutting tooling. Key behaviors:

  • Sealed front volume ("pressure chamber"): below the first acoustic resonance, eardrum pressure follows diaphragm displacement — why sealed headphones/IEMs deliver flat response to 20 Hz and below, something no small speaker in a room can do.
  • Leaks kill bass predictably: a leak forms a high-pass with the front-volume compliance. Products are either leak-tolerant (pre-vented + ANC compensation, e.g., AirPods Pro) or seal-dependent (max bass, fit-critical).
  • Rear vents set damping and bass Q: resistive mesh flattens the fundamental resonance; port area/length tunes a Helmholtz resonance to reinforce or shape bass.

5.2 The high-frequency (modal) region

Above ~1–2 kHz, lumped models break down: the front volume develops standing waves and pad shape becomes a waveguide. Techniques:

  • Angled drivers/baffles aim at the canal like a near-field speaker — smooths 2–6 kHz, improves imaging (Sennheiser HD 600 lineage, Audeze).
  • Waveguides & lenses control the first modes (Focal "M" domes; Dan Clark AMTS tuned resonators absorbing targeted treble modes).
  • Damping schemes: felt, wool, foam, micro-perforated sheets placed at pressure maxima; materials specified by flow resistance (MKS rayls).
  • Pad acoustics: depth sets front-volume size; opening shapes pinna interaction; leather (sealed/reflective) vs. velour (leaky/lossy) trades bass for treble smoothness. Changing pads is changing the headphone — and pad wear is a major cause of unit drift over years.

5.3 IEM-specific acoustics

  • The eartip-to-eardrum column has a half-wave resonance (~7–9 kHz) set by insertion depth — deep insertion pushes it out of the sensitive range (the Etymotic philosophy).
  • Nozzle bore diameter and internal tube networks act as acoustic filters; dampers tame BA spout peaks.
  • Front vents relieve occlusion and static pressure ("driver flex"); rear vents tune dynamic-driver bass.
  • Tip material (silicone vs. foam) changes seal and treble absorption — a tuning variable handed to the user.

5.4 Passive isolation

Isolation is a mass-spring problem: cup mass + pad compliance against the head. Closed over-ears manage ~10–25 dB above 1 kHz but little below 200 Hz (that's ANC's job); deep-sealing foam-tip IEMs manage 25–40 dB broadband — the best passive isolation available.

Chapter 6

Frequency Response & Target Curves

6.1 Why "flat" is wrong at the eardrum

A loudspeaker calibrated flat in an anechoic room arrives at the eardrum with the body's own gain: torso reflections, pinna gain, and canal resonance produce a large hump near 3 kHz. A headphone measured flat at the eardrum would sound dull and wrong — it must reproduce those features. Hence targets:

TargetBasisCharacter / status
Free-field (old DIN/IEC)Frontal anechoic source at eardrumToo bright/lean for most listeners.
Diffuse-field (Theile, 1986)Energy from all directions, averagedClassic studio reference (Etymotic, older AKG/Beyer tunings).
Harman (Olive/Welti, 2013–19)Preference-tuned + validated with large controlled tests≈ DF + bass shelf (~+6 dB <100 Hz over-ear; more in-ear), relaxed treble. Separate OE 2018 / IE 2019 curves. Preference studies: ~64% "Harman-like" majority; 2024–25 follow-ups on newer fixtures found ~72% still preferring Harman-style tunings.
5128-era targets (JM-1, DF+tilt)B&K 5128 anatomically-correct fixtureFixture DF response + preference tilt (~−0.8 to −1.6 dB/oct) and adjustable bass shelf, rather than one fixed curve.
Spatial-audio caveatAES research, 2025Binaural content already encodes HRTF filtering — a different (roughly DF-flat) target may be more correct for spatial playback.

6.2 How designers use targets

  1. Choose fixture + target for the product class (e.g., "Harman-2018 within ±2 dB, 20 Hz–10 kHz").
  2. Tune acoustically as close as possible (pads, damping, vents — EQ-by-construction).
  3. For DSP products, finish with fixed or adaptive digital EQ; for passive products, acoustics is all you get.
  4. Validate with blind listening panels — a target is a statistical preference center, not gospel; deviations are the "house sound" (Sennheiser's warm-neutral, Beyerdynamic's treble lift, Sony consumer bass).

6.3 Reading a measurement

  • Deviations <±1 dB over broad bands: subtle. ±3 dB in the ear-gain region: an obvious character change.
  • Narrow spikes above 8 kHz on a coupler are often fixture artifacts — trust ranges, not single points, up top.
  • Bass measured on a flat plate vs. a head with pinna and leak simulation differs hugely — always know the fixture.

Plus anechoic chambers for leakage, ISO 4869 / ANSI S12.42 setups for isolation and ANC attenuation, and head-tracker rigs for spatial-audio latency.

7.2 The core measurement set

  • Frequency response vs. target; L/R matching.
  • THD vs. frequency and level: dynamics rise in bass at high SPL; good planars stay <0.1–0.5% at 94–100 dB. Low-order harmonics are surprisingly tolerable; intermodulation and rub/buzz are not.
  • Impedance & phase vs. frequency: reveals resonances and crossovers, predicts source interaction.
  • Sensitivity (dB SPL at 1 mW or 1 V).
  • Isolation and leakage (office-etiquette spec for consumer products).
  • Time domain: impulse response, CSD "waterfall" for ringing, group delay/latency (>~20 ms breaks lip-sync uncompensated; gaming targets <40 ms end-to-end).
  • Rub & buzz QC: 100% end-of-line swept-sine artifact detection (Klippel / Listen SoundCheck), seconds per unit.
  • Wireless metrics: codec latency, OTA antenna performance, mic polar/noise, sidetone, ANC stability margins across fits.

7.3 Preference prediction

Harman's research produced regression models predicting listener preference from FR-deviation statistics (slope, smoothness, bass/treble deviation) — the basis of "predicted preference scores" in review databases. They are screening tools; exotic designs fall outside model validity.

Chapter 8

Electrical Design: Impedance, Sensitivity, Amplification

8.1 Impedance

  • Ranges from ~8 Ω (some IEMs) to 600 Ω (classic Beyerdynamic studio models).
  • Why high impedance exists: thinner coil wire → lighter coil → better treble behavior; plus broadcast-desk history and voltage-noise immunity.
  • Source interaction: output impedance forms a divider with the headphone's frequency-dependent impedance. Rule of thumb: source ≤ ⅛ of headphone impedance. Multi-BA IEMs with 8→60 Ω swings audibly change tone on high-impedance sources.

8.2 Sensitivity & power budget

To hit 110 dB peaks on a 100 dB/mW headphone requires 10 mW; on an 83 dB/mW planar it takes ~500 mW — hence "needs an amp." Phone dongles deliver ~1 Vrms / 30–60 mW; desktop amps deliver 1–10 W into low loads. Convert specs carefully: 100 dB/mW @ 32 Ω ≈ 115 dB/V.

8.3 Cables, connectors, balanced drive

  • Measurable cable effects are essentially resistance (level/damping) and extreme-case capacitance for IEMs; most audible "cable differences" trace to fit, seal, and level changes.
  • Connectors: 3.5 mm / 6.35 mm TRS, 4.4 mm Pentaconn balanced, 2-pin 0.78 mm and MMCX for IEMs, USB-C digital.
  • Balanced drive doubles voltage swing and removes shared-ground crosstalk — matters for hard loads and long runs, not inherently "better sound."
  • Cable microphonics is a real ergonomic spec — solved with over-ear routing and soft jackets.

8.4 DACs and sources

Modern converter performance (SNR >110 dB, THD <0.001%) exceeds transducer limits; audible source differences today usually come from output impedance, power limits, DSP/EQ, and lossy-codec chains — not converter quality.

Chapter 9

Wireless, DSP & Active Noise Cancellation

Modern consumer headphones are DSP-defined products: acoustics provide raw capability; software delivers the shipped sound.

9.1 Bluetooth audio

  • Codecs: SBC (baseline), AAC (Apple ecosystem), aptX family (Adaptive/Lossless), LDAC (to 990 kbps), LHDC, and LE Audio's LC3 (better quality per bit, lower latency, Auracast broadcast). All classic-BT codecs are lossy in practice.
  • Latency: SBC/AAC ~150–250 ms; game modes/LC3 reach ~40–80 ms; true lip-sync-transparent wireless needs proprietary 2.4 GHz dongles (<20 ms).
  • System design: SoC selection (Qualcomm, Airoha, BES), antennas inside an RF-hostile head-adjacent enclosure, ear-to-ear links, multipoint, battery (TWS 5–8 h + case; over-ears 30–60+ h with ANC).

9.2 Active Noise Cancellation

TopologyMicsBandwidthCharacter
FeedforwardExternal — hears noise before it entersMostly <1 kHzCancels what it predicts; wrong prediction = added noise; wind-sensitive.
FeedbackInternal, near driver — hears what the ear hearsBroader low-mid coverage; corrects fit variationStability risk (howl) — a classic control-loop problem.
HybridBothBest depth (30–45 dB @ 100–300 Hz in leaders) + robustnessTwo good mic sets + serious DSP; today's premium standard.
  • The secondary path (driver→ear transfer) changes with every fit; adaptive filters (FxLMS family) and per-ear calibration keep the loop optimal.
  • ANC only works well below ~1–2 kHz — above that, wavelengths are too short for one-point cancellation; passive isolation must handle the highs. Total attenuation = passive + active.
  • Side-effects to engineer around: "eardrum suck" pressure sensation, wind noise on feedforward mics, mic/ADC self-noise floor, occlusion compensation.
  • Transparency/hear-through is ANC's inverse — reproducing outside sound naturally, with own-voice handling. Arguably harder than cancelling.

9.3 The DSP feature stack

  • Corrective/creative EQ, psychoacoustic bass, dynamic equal-loudness compensation.
  • Adaptive fit compensation: the feedback mic measures in-ear response and auto-EQs bass to seal quality (Apple, Bose, Sony all ship variants).
  • Spatial audio: binaural rendering with IMU head tracking; personalized HRTF from ear photos/scans.
  • Hearing personalization/assist: audiogram-based gain — now regulated OTC hearing-aid territory in the U.S.
  • Voice: beamforming arrays, bone-conduction voice sensors, AI noise suppression for calls.
Chapter 10

Ergonomics & Industrial Design

A headphone is a wearable clamped to the most measured and most variable part of the body. Comfort research is now anthropometrics-driven, using 3D head-scan databases across populations.

10.1 Key anthropometric variables

Head breadth and height, bitragion arc, ear position, pinna dimensions (~55–70 mm typical adult height range), ear-canal size/angle (drives S/M/L tip sizing), and population differences that make single-geometry designs fit unevenly across global markets.

10.2 Clamping force & pressure distribution

  • Practical over-ear comfort window: roughly 3–6 N total clamp (on a 145–155 mm jig). Above ~5–6 N most users fatigue within an hour; below ~2 N stability and bass seal suffer. On-ears tolerate less — pressure lands on cartilage.
  • Pressure = force ÷ contact area: comfort correlates with peak pressure distribution, not raw clamp. Bigger, deeper, softer pads spread load; the pinna must never touch the grille.
  • Weight: under ~250 g "disappears"; 300–400 g needs excellent headband load-spreading; >450 g (some planars) limits session length regardless. Neck torque from cup depth matters, not just grams.
  • Thermal comfort: leather pads seal heat and moisture; fabric breathes but leaks bass — a genuine acoustic-vs-comfort trade.

10.3 Mechanism design

Headband sliders (detented, smooth, self-adjusting), yoke rotation/swivel for cheek-plane conformity, folding hinges (a classic fatigue-failure site), strain reliefs, pad attachment (snap rings, magnets, adhesive — determines user-replaceability). Every joint is a creak risk next to a microphone-grade transducer: mechanical-noise specs are real.

10.4 Industrial design & CMF

Forged carbon, anodized aluminum, magnesium (light + damped), sustainable fabrics. Iconic design languages — AirPods' white stem, Beats' "b", the HD 800's silver lattice — show industrial design is brand strategy.

Controls: buttons vs. capacitive touch vs. rotating crowns. On TWS, the control surface is the transducer enclosure, so touch taps must be mechanically decoupled or DSP-muted.

10.5 IEM/TWS fit specifics

Concha-lock geometry, forward-down nozzle angles, stem vs. stemless mass distribution, IPX ratings for sport, and eartip science (silicone durometer, foam recovery, flange counts). Custom IEMs go further: ear impressions or 3D scans printed into acrylic/silicone shells — the fit ceiling, at the cost of resale and re-fits as ears change.

Chapter 11

Materials & Manufacturing

11.1 Diaphragm materials — the heart

Figure of merit: specific modulus (stiffness ÷ density) plus internal damping. Roughly by exotic-ness: PET/Mylar → PEN/PEEK multilayers → LCP → bio-cellulose → metal foils (Al, Ti, Mg) → beryllium (highest useful specific modulus; toxic to machine, costly) → coatings (DLC, CNT, graphene-doped — marketing frequently outruns physics; a "graphene driver" is usually a polymer film with a coating).

11.2 Magnets & motors

Sintered NdFeB (N48–N55) dominates; grades trade flux vs. temperature stability. Planars consume large magnet volumes (their cost + weight driver). Ferrite persists in budget lines. Assembly demands magnetized-part automation and gap cleanliness — a steel filing in a 0.5 mm gap is a warranty return.

11.3 Enclosures & structure

Injection-molded ABS/PC (glass-filled for stiffness), CNC or stamped aluminum, die-cast magnesium (superb stiffness/damping/weight for yokes and baffles), stainless headband springs, 3D-printed acrylic/nylon for custom-IEM shells. Acoustic meshes (steel, nylon, MPP) are specified by rayl value like electronic components.

11.4 The production line

  1. Driver plantDiaphragm thermoforming, coil winding and bonding, magnet charging, automated assembly. Every driver is measured and graded, then matched into L/R pairs (±0.5–1 dB for premium lines).
  2. Final assemblyCup assembly with damping placement (surprisingly manual and skill-dependent), pads, headband join, cabling or board drop-in for wireless.
  3. End-of-line QC100% acoustic test in mini-chambers: FR mask, rub & buzz, polarity, mic checks. Sampling audits and batch torture tests.
  4. Reliability qualificationDrop, tumble, cable flex (>10k cycles), headband flex (>50k), sweat/salt-fog, UV, −20/+60 °C soak, pad-wear simulation, button life; for TWS: case-hinge life, battery cycles, IP water-tank verification.
Cost anatomy (typical consumer wireless over-ear, illustrative): transducers are a minor line item — a few dollars. The money is in SoC/battery/mics, mechanics/CMF, certification, and the software team.
Chapter 12

The End-to-End Design Process

A realistic 12–24 month program for a serious headphone:

  1. DefinitionUser, use case, price tier, target curve, isolation/ANC goals, weight/battery/latency budgets — the product requirements document every later trade-off refers to.
  2. ArchitectureForm factor, open/closed, driver technology and size, passive vs. DSP-corrected, platform SoC. Simulation begins: lumped-element acoustics + magnetics FEA (COMSOL/JMAG) + early BEM.
  3. Mule prototypesExisting cups + new drivers, 3D-printed baffles, hand-cut damping — dozens of iterations chasing the target. Experienced acousticians speak in "add 2 rayls behind the driver" moves.
  4. Alpha / EVTFirst tooled parts, real electronics; DSP tuning against golden acoustic units; ANC loop-shaping across a fit-variation corpus (many heads, glasses, hair).
  5. Beta / DVTDesign freeze; blind panels vs. competitors; reliability quals; certifications (FCC/CE/BT SIG, EN 50332 / EN 62368 hearing-safety limits).
  6. PVT & rampLine yields, test-station correlation to lab fixtures, driver-matching statistics — the quiet battle of keeping unit #100,000 sounding like the golden sample.
  7. Post-launchFirmware feature cycle (modern headphones improve after purchase), pad/tip spares, telemetry-driven tuning updates.
The team: electroacoustic engineers, DSP/embedded engineers, mechanical engineers, industrial designers, perceptual-audio scientists, reliability and test engineers, firmware/app software. Great headphones are credited to tuners but shipped by systems teams.
Chapter 13

Hearing Safety, Standards & Regulation

  • Noise-induced hearing loss is cumulative and permanent. WHO/NIOSH guidance: ~85 dB(A) for 8 h max, halving time per +3 dB — 100 dB(A) is minutes, not hours. Isolation (passive or ANC) is a safety feature: it lets users listen quieter.
  • EN 50332 limits portable output in the EU (~85 dB(A) warnings, 100 dB(A) caps with acknowledgment); phones implement dosage warnings.
  • Key standards: IEC 60268-7 (headphone measurement), IEC 60318 (ear simulators), ITU-T P.57, ANSI/ASA S3.7, EN 62368-1 (product/battery safety), ISO 4869 (attenuation), BT SIG qualification, regional RF (FCC/CE/MIC/SRRC), and FDA OTC hearing-aid rules (2022) which pulled hearables into regulated territory.
  • Batteries: li-po cells near the head demand rigorous charge management, cell qualification, and UN 38.3 transport certification.
Chapter 14

Sustainability & Repairability

Failure reality: batteries and pads die long before drivers. Glued-in TWS batteries made earbuds a disposable category; regulatory pressure (EU battery regulation) and design responses (Fairphone Fairbuds' replaceable cells; user-replaceable pads/cables in studio lines like HD 25/HD 600) point the way.

  • Design levers: modularity (pads/cables/batteries), standard connectors over proprietary, firmware-support lifespan commitments, recycled plastics/aluminum, repair documentation and parts supply.
  • Longevity is also acoustic: replaceable pads restore original tuning; sealed pad designs guarantee drift.
Chapter 15

Practical Exercises

  1. Ear-gain safariPlay pink noise through speakers, then any headphone; use a parametric EQ to find how much 3 kHz boost/cut makes the headphone match the speakers' timbre. You've just measured your personal ear-gain error.
  2. Leak labLift a pad edge 2 mm while playing a 40 Hz tone, then 1 kHz. Quantify the bass-only loss — the leak high-pass in action. Repeat wearing glasses.
  3. Pad swap experimentCompare worn vs. new pads (or leather vs. velour) and log the tonal change region by region.
  4. Target-curve tourUse AutoEQ presets to A/B one IEM against Harman IE 2019, diffuse-field, and flat-at-eardrum; note which regions cause "shouty," "muddy," "dull."
  5. Impedance interactionDrive a multi-BA IEM from a low-impedance dongle, then through a ~30 Ω series resistor; describe the tonal shift via the impedance-vs-frequency idea.
  6. Occlusion demoEarplugs deep vs. shallow: hum and chew; note the low-frequency boom shift — then find the vent on any ANC earbud and reason about its job.
  7. Build oneDIY kits (T50RP modding, 3D-printed IEM shells with 10 mm dynamics + mesh dampers) deliver the tune-measure loop firsthand for under $100.
  8. Blind panelRun a level-matched (±0.5 dB — critical!) blind A/B with three friends; discover how loudness and expectation dominate sighted impressions.

Glossary

BA
Balanced armature — miniature pivoting-reed transducer used in IEMs and hearing aids.
Break-up
Region where a diaphragm stops moving as a piston and flexes in modes.
Clamping force
Total inward headband force, in newtons.
Coupler / HATS
Standardized artificial ear / head-and-torso simulator for measurement.
CSD
Cumulative spectral decay ("waterfall") — spectral decay over time; reveals ringing.
Diffuse field
Sound field with equal energy from all directions; basis of the DF target.
Ear gain
The ~2–4 kHz boost the outer ear adds to frontal sound; must exist in headphone tuning.
FxLMS
Adaptive filtering algorithm family behind adaptive ANC.
Harman target
Preference-derived eardrum response curve (OE 2018 / IE 2019 versions).
HRTF
Head-related transfer function — direction-dependent ear/torso filtering.
Occlusion effect
Boomy amplification of one's own body sounds when the canal is blocked.
Pinna
The visible outer ear.
Pressure chamber effect
Sealed-volume bass-extension mechanism unique to headphones.
Rayl
Unit of specific acoustic flow resistance; how damping meshes are specified.
Sensitivity
SPL produced per milliwatt or per volt.
THD
Total harmonic distortion.
TWS
True wireless stereo earbuds.
